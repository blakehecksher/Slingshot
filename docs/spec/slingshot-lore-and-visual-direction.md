# Slingshot - Lore And Visual Direction

_Status: active_
_Last updated: 2026-05-14 2355_

## Purpose

This document defines the spiritual, lore, naming, and aesthetic direction for Slingshot.

The active game is a gravity time-trial racer. Lore should support the racing loop, course identity, ship feel, HUD language, and environmental art direction. It should not pull the current build back toward mining, cargo, economy, combat, or ship-upgrade systems unless those systems are explicitly revived later.

## Core premise

The asteroid field contains **Dead Iron**: a rare ultra-dense gravitational material that distorts local space far beyond what its visible mass should allow.

Most asteroids are ordinary rock. The dangerous ones contain buried veins, pockets, or compressed cores of Dead Iron. These deposits make certain asteroids behave like gravity wells, pulling ships off-course, bending trajectories, trapping careless pilots, and turning open space into navigable terrain.

The field is not just where the game happens. The field is the road, the hazard, and the reason the race exists.

The central question remains:

> How close can you get?

In the current game, the answer is measured in lap times, split deltas, leaderboard rank, and how aggressively a pilot can skim gravity wells without wrecking.

## Field Backstory

For years, the field was treated as junk rock: too unstable for settlement, too remote for conventional work, and too dangerous for normal survey crews. Then drones started disappearing into impossible trajectories. Some were later found crushed against ordinary-looking asteroids; others were trapped in slow decaying orbits around rocks that should not have had enough mass to matter.

The first serious surveys discovered Dead Iron. After that, the field stopped being ignored. Claims, crews, salvagers, mechanics, patrols, and timing boards followed. Racing grew out of that working culture: the same skill that kept pilots alive near heavy rocks became sport, wager, reputation, and obsession.

## The racing scene

Slingshot is not clean futuristic racing. It is a dangerous working-field racing scene built out of mining equipment, salvage culture, and rough competition.

The races grew out of the field economy. Pilots, mechanics, claim crews, and salvage operators already had to fly close to Dead Iron wells to survive. Eventually the same skill became sport, wager, reputation, and obsession.

The racing culture should feel informal, practical, and a little unsafe:

- repurposed mining lanes
- claim-board timing records
- depot terminals
- rough course beacons
- patched ships
- local rivalries
- field crews watching times between jobs
- pilots treating danger like a craft

## Dead Iron

Dead Iron is valuable because it behaves wrongly.

It appears as dark metallic veins, dense nodules, glassy inclusions, and buried cores. When concentrated, compressed, or aligned in large enough masses, it produces gravitational effects disproportionate to its apparent size.

A practical field rule:

> Dead Iron is safest when dispersed and most dangerous when concentrated.

This explains why two asteroids of similar visible size can behave very differently. One may be mostly rock. Another may contain a buried core dense enough to bend flight paths from a distance.

Pilots learn to read Dead Iron the way sailors read currents.

## Tone

This is not a clean sci-fi frontier. It is a working field.

The field should feel like a half-legal industrial rush at the edge of known space. There are corporate claims, independent pilots, scavengers, old pros, repair crews, timing crews, wreck chasers, and patrol ships that enforce the rules only when paid to care.

The mood is functional, worn, and specific. Ships are not sleek hero machines. They are tools modified, repaired, overloaded, stripped down, and flown too hard.

The depot is not a grand space station. It is a practical foothold in low-gravity open space: fuel, repairs, parts, timing boards, rumors, debt, bad coffee, and a launch path back into danger.

## Visual direction

Slingshot should feel like a dangerous working-field racing scene built out of mining equipment, salvage culture, and rough competition.

Reference sensibility:

- Cowboy Bebop in spirit, not literal anime
- functional ships with jobs
- patched hardware
- worn paint
- exposed thrusters
- industrial race equipment
- old terminals and practical displays
- a field economy that has been repurposed into competition

Palette direction:

- desaturated warm metal
- rust
- off-white
- deep navy
- amber
- black iron
- dirty gray
- muted teal/green instrument accents

Avoid:

- pristine chrome sci-fi
- neon cyberpunk
- generic space opera
- luxury racing gloss
- purple/blue gradient futurism
- giant marketing hero sections
- decorative UI elements that do not imply a function

## Asteroid visual language

Dead Iron-rich asteroids should be readable before the HUD explains them.

Possible signs:

- dark metallic seams across the asteroid surface
- exposed black or blue-black mineral faces
- glassy impact scars around dense deposits
- dust rings or small captured debris fields
- subtle orbiting fragments around high-gravity rocks
- warm instrument glow or scan shimmer near exposed veins
- surface cracks that imply something heavier inside

The strongest asteroids should feel ancient, heavy, and wrong.

## Course setting language

### Open Claim Space

Low gravity, safer lanes, wider routes, sparse asteroid clusters.

Use for starter courses and training.

Visual notes:

- clearer starfield
- fewer hazards
- depot/base visible in distance if it does not clutter racing
- smaller rocks
- lighter dust
- warmer instrument colors

Gameplay feeling:

- learn gates
- learn boost
- learn trajectory minimap
- forgiving slingshots

### Dead Iron Belt

Moderate to high gravity, darker asteroids, richer course rhythm.

Visual notes:

- blue-black metallic seams
- red-orange fissures
- captured debris
- dusty rings
- stronger light contrast
- industrial checkpoint hardware

Gameplay feeling:

- committed slingshot lines
- meaningful mistakes
- denser route reading

### Black Core Field

Deep-field racing around massive gravity wells.

Visual notes:

- huge ancient asteroids
- black metallic cores
- glassy scars
- oppressive scale
- dimmer ambient light
- stronger rumble/vibration language
- warning glows and unstable dust

Gameplay feeling:

- speed required to survive
- risky close passes
- intense split-second trajectory decisions

## Race hardware and gates

Checkpoint gates should not feel like clean arcade rings. They should look like rugged navigation and race equipment adapted from field hardware.

Good gate signals:

- beacon struts
- bolted emitter nodes
- amber/green signal lights
- worn metal frames
- holographic center ring
- warning strobes
- antenna fins
- maintenance markings
- visible power modules

Gate readability matters more than decoration:

- required gate should be unmistakable
- upcoming gates should be visible but secondary
- passed gates should fade clearly
- off-screen guidance should share the required-gate color language

Current gameplay color language:

- active/required gate: green
- upcoming gates: amber
- passed gates: faint neutral

## Ships

Ships should look like working-field craft converted into racing machines.

They are not luxury racecars and not generic fighter jets. They are repurposed field ships, stripped claim craft, custom runners, and dangerous homebuilt machines.

Good ship signals:

- oversized maneuvering thrusters
- exposed reaction-control clusters
- reinforced nose cages
- external engine pods
- radiator fins
- sensor booms
- patched armor
- worn paint
- visible cable runs
- asymmetric repairs
- clear silhouette from chase/cockpit context

Avoid:

- smooth chrome ships
- ornamental wings with no function
- designs that do not show how the ship turns, boosts, or survives stress
- ship variety that distracts from the standardized racing loop

## Sound and feedback

Dead Iron should be felt before it is explained.

The closer the ship gets to a strong well, the more the world should communicate strain:

- low hull groan
- subsonic rumble
- cockpit vibration
- controller rumble
- warning tones that feel mechanical, not sterile
- audio pressure around dangerous close passes
- silence snapping back in safer open space

The sound of the field is part of the HUD.

A skilled player should eventually know when they are too close because the ship sounds wrong.

## Names and language

The world should use plain, workmanlike names more than polished sci-fi terms.

Good naming direction:

- Dead Iron
- Anchor Metal
- wellstone
- black nickel
- dead core
- heavy rock
- well field
- bad orbit
- hot pass
- soft capture
- deep run
- claim rock
- wreck drift
- timing board
- ghost line
- red pass

Avoid overly clean technobabble unless a specific corporation or instrument readout would use it.

A field pilot should not say, "The exotic gravimetric substrate is destabilizing."

A field pilot should say, "That rock is pulling hard."

## Design principles

1. **Gravity is the main terrain.** The player is not flying through empty space with rocks in it. They are flying through a field of forces.
2. **Closeness is skill.** The game is about getting close enough to exploit danger without being captured by it.
3. **Richer visual danger should imply stronger gravity.** The player should learn to read the field visually.
4. **Ships should show function.** Thrusters, sensors, armor, and wear should explain how the craft survives the field.
5. **The world should feel used.** This is not pristine science fiction. It is a working, worn, risky culture.
6. **Lore supports readability.** Mystery is allowed, but the player-facing rules should stay clear.
